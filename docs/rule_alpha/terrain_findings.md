# What terrain does Layer 1 actually leave behind?

A survey, not a change.  Nothing in the planner was touched to produce it.
Numbers come from `reports/rule_alpha/terrain.json`, pictures from
`reports/rule_alpha/images/*/c*_terrain.png`, both generated by
`scripts/rule_alpha_terrain.py`.  All figures below are container 0 of each
scenario, analytic model, end of Layer 1.

## The target being measured against

| zone | intent |
|---|---|
| centre | normal-hard terraces, higher at the back, lower at the front |
| shelf | first home for soft / priority / SP — spend the headroom before the floor |
| left/right back | big or tall hard; the outer anchor that keeps big items out of the centre |
| left/right front | low, fairly large hard: a landing pad for soft / priority overflow, ordinary support if none arrives |
| front centre | accessibility first — blocking it must cost more than blocking the front corners |
| chamfer | the progressive wedge staircase, as a local support-growth mechanism |

The reporting partition is 4 × 2 over the usable floor (`chamfer` / `left` /
`centre` / `right` in x; `back` / `front` split at mid-depth) plus the shelf.
It is deliberately *not* the zone set the rules steer by, so the tables can show
where the two have drifted apart.

## Headline: the four things that are actually wrong

1. **Normal-hard goes to the edges, not the centre.**  Mean centre share
   **0.25**, mean edge share **0.53**.  The intended arrangement is the other
   way round.
2. **The terrain is not a terrace.**  Only **4 of 10** boards rise monotonically
   front → back, and the height histogram is bimodal — bare floor, or one box
   high — with very little in between.
3. **The way in closes.**  Mean reachable-at-floor **0.20** of the plan, and
   **0.054** of the back half.  **42 %** of all remaining bare floor is
   *stranded*: empty, but walled off.
4. **The shelf is barely used.**  Mean fill of the headroom volume **0.24**, and
   three boards finish with a completely empty shelf — one of them
   (`05`) having pushed two priority items onto the floor instead.

Everything below is the evidence.

**The back bias is real.**  y-centroid is above 0.5 on every board, and back

**But `left-back` is used exactly once, in all twelve scenarios.**  Every other

box.  *The container does not have a symmetric left/right perimeter.*  Any
zoning that says "left and right back get the tall hard cargo" has to be
rewritten for this ULD as "**right** back gets the tall hard cargo, the left is
the chamfer's".

**Tall items leak to the front.**  `chamfer-front` and `right-front` collect
tall-perimeter items in 7 of 12 scenarios, at steps 4–17.  That is what closes
the approach — see §5.

## Reading: where should each fix live?

**Layer 1 should change for two of the six, and only two.**

*Keep in Layer 1 — the approach.*  §5 is not something Layer 2 can repair: once
the floor is closed it is closed, and Layer 2 arriving to find 42 % of the free
floor stranded has already lost that volume.  The cost has to be paid at the
moment the blocking item is placed, which is Layer 1's decision.  It also fits
the machinery that already exists: this is the same *option pricing* used for
the wedge strip — placing at `front-centre` destroys the approach to everything
behind it, which is irreversible, while leaving it costs only the volume held
now.  The measurement it needs is exactly `reach_report`: how much still-open
area would this placement cut off.

*Keep in Layer 1 — the shelf.*  §4 says the shelf is under-filled because only
one layer of shelf items ever goes up, and `05` says priority cargo reaches the
floor with an empty shelf above it.  Both are Layer 1 routing bugs, cheap to
fix, and independent of any Layer 2 design.

*Push to Layer 2 — the terrain shape.*  §1, §2, §6 all describe the same thing:
the surface is lumpy, edge-heavy and not terraced.  Trying to fix that by
re-weighting Layer 1's zone preferences would mean asking one greedy pass to
build a shape whose value only appears later.  It is the wrong place.  The
edge bias in particular is not obviously wrong *as a first layer* — big boxes
against walls is a reasonable base — it is only wrong relative to a terrace
plan that does not exist yet.  Build the terrace in Layer 2, on top of what
Layer 1 leaves, and judge Layer 1 by whether it leaves a usable base.

*Rewrite the zoning itself — left ≠ right.*  §2 and §3 show the target zoning's
"left/right back" and "left/right front" symmetry does not survive contact with
this ULD.  The chamfer eats the left band.  The zoning should be restated
asymmetrically: **right** perimeter is the tall-hard anchor, the **left** is the
chamfer's staircase, and only the right front is a genuine overflow pad.

*Guard, don't zone — soft capping.*  `06` and `05` show the real risk is not
where soft goes but that it **caps the whole surface**.  A cheap Layer 1 rule
— never let hard-top area fall below some floor while hard cargo remains in the
stream — is worth more than any amount of zone tuning.

## Generalising `support → controlled overhang → new support`

Yes, and the wedge is a special case of it rather than the other way round.

In the staircase, reach is `min(chamfer limit, f·w)`: a *geometric* boundary and
a *stability* boundary.  In the centre there is no chamfer, so only the
stability limit survives — and the thing worth reaching over is different.

**In the wedge, overhang buys volume the floor cannot reach.  In the centre,
overhang buys *flatness*.**  That is the generalisation worth making.  A gap
between two boxes that is too narrow for any item is dead at floor level, but a
box spanning it — supported by both neighbours, unsupported over the gap — is
legal as long as the support ratio holds, and its top is a *single plateau
wider than either box below it*.  Each layer can therefore be flatter than the
one under it.  That is precisely the terrace-growth mechanism §6 says is
missing, and it is bought by the same trick.

Concretely, the three pieces already exist:

* **support** — the height map, with `support_type` telling hard from soft.  A
  candidate's support ratio is the fraction of its footprint whose top is
  within contact tolerance of its underside; the official floor is 0.6, and
  `wedge_overhang_fraction` = 0.25 is the conservative version already in use.
* **controlled overhang** — the unsupported fraction, capped the same way.  The
  wedge caps it against the chamfer plane; the centre caps it against nothing,
  so only the stability term binds.
* **new support** — score the *plateau merge*: how much larger the resulting
  contiguous same-height hard plateau is than the largest one it was built
  from.  In the wedge the analogous quantity is `next_step_gain`; here it is
  a flatness gain rather than a reach gain.

Two cautions the survey makes concrete, both of which the wedge version did not
have to face:

* **Soft tops are not support.**  §6 shows boards where the entire built
  surface is soft or priority.  A bridge needs *hard* support under both ends,
  so the mechanism's availability depends on a hard-top area that Layer 1 does
  not currently guarantee.  That is the coupling between the "guard, don't
  zone" fix above and this mechanism working at all.
* **Bridging and reachability fight each other.**  A bridge at the front is
  exactly the wall that §5 measures.  So the same option price has to apply:
  the merge gain must be worth the approach it cuts off.  Building bridges
  back-to-front makes them nearly free, which is a second, independent reason
  the back-first order matters.

One more thing the terrain says about the terrace direction.  The user's
"higher at the back, lower at the front" is not only a stacking preference — §5
shows it is the *transport* condition.  A surface that is monotone
non-increasing towards the opening is one where every terrace can still be
reached by a straight-in sweep at its own height; the moment the front is
higher than the back, everything behind it is sealed.  The two boards that fall
towards the back (`02`, `12`) are exactly the boards a Layer 2 would struggle
with most.  So the terrace shape can be justified as a hard constraint rather
than a heuristic, and `reach_report` is the function that scores it.
